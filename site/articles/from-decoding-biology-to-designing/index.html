From Decoding Biology to Designing It

For years, AI has helped us understand how living systems work. Now, it is beginning to create them.

By GAMGEEAugust 17, 20263 min read

At Gamgee, we use AI to design personalized cancer vaccines for dogs. While we are focused on that mission, we want to also highlight some other amazing science we are seeing in the field, like developing bacteriophages computationally. In other words, developing bacteriophages with AI rather than in a research lab.

Researchers at Stanford and the Arc Institute recently designed 16 fully functioning bacteriophages capable of infecting and destroying target bacteria like E. coli with AI.

Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacteria rather than human cells, and scientists have long been interested in their potential to combat harmful bacteria, including strains that are resistant to traditional antibiotics.

What makes this research particularly interesting is that scientists aren’t simply using AI to study an existing biological system. They are beginning to use AI to generate new biological designs that can then be built and tested in the lab.

Using genomic foundation models including Evo 2, which was trained on trillions of nucleotides to understand and generate DNA sequences, researchers created and tested hundreds of new bacteriophage designs.

16 of these designs successfully infected and killed target E. coli strains showing that we can now write a virus’s genetic code on a computer and biologically “print” it and use it to destroy real bacteria. You can read more in Stanford’s overview of the study.

The number itself is impressive, but the bigger story is the rapid advancement and vast possibilities in computational biology.

AI in biology has largely been associated with understanding and predicting. Models can help researchers analyze enormous amounts of biological data, predict molecular structures, understand mutations, and identify patterns.

Now we’re beginning to see the next step: design.

For precision medicine, design is a powerful move because computational models could help us understand how we can create effective therapeutics around a specific patient.

Every patient’s biology is different. In cancer specifically, every tumor can contain its own unique collection of mutations. Those differences create an opportunity toward therapies designed around the molecular characteristics of an individual patient.

At Gamgee, we are leveraging this shift by building an end-to-end platform for computationally designed, personalized mRNA cancer vaccines for dogs. By using AI, we can identify unique mutations to computationally engineer a precise, individualized vaccine.

The goal is to take the biological information contained within an individual dog’s tumor and use computational tools to help determine which mutations are most relevant for a personalized immune response. Those insights can then inform the design of an mRNA vaccine created specifically for that patient. You can learn more about Gamgee’s process here.

This is what makes the evolution of generative biology so exciting.

Whether researchers are computationally designing bacteriophages, proteins, genetic sequences, or personalized therapeutics, we are seeing the same fundamental shift: biology is increasingly becoming something we can not only understand, but create.

There is still a long way to go. Computationally generated biology still needs to be experimentally validated, and successful designs have to work in the complexity of real biological systems. But the ability to generate candidates computationally could dramatically expand the number of biological possibilities scientists are able to explore.

We’re entering the era of generative biology, where computational models don’t just interpret biology, but help us predict it, design it, and engineer it for a purpose.
