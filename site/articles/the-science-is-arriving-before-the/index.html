The Science Is Arriving Before the System Is Ready

Earlier this month, I spent a few days at the AVMA Convention 2026 in Anaheim, California.

By GAMGEEJuly 31, 20263 min read

The American Veterinary Medical Association Convention (AVMA) is one of the few places where you can see entire veterinary system at once: general practitioners, specialists, technicians, researchers, diagnostic labs, pharmaceutical companies, software vendors and startups.

GAMGEE is new. The name is new, the science we are building is new, the company is new, and I’m definitely new to veterinary medicine. I went to meet people, but also to listen and get a feel for where the industry is going.

Walking around AVMA, I kept returning to the same thought: The science is arriving before the system is ready for it.

This is something we are not used to in Silicon Valley, where people are always clamoring for the next new thing. Veterinary science is rightly very evidence and outcome based so the road from something being theoretically possible to it actually helping patients is going to be an uphill climb

Veterinary medicine is still massively under-digitized

That may sound like an odd conclusion to draw in an exhibit hall filled with cloud software, imaging systems, connected analyzers and AI products.

But under-digitized doesn’t mean veterinary practices have no technology. It means the technology is fragmented. There are plenty of tools. They just don’t always speak to each other, which means people are still doing a lot of the connecting by hand.

That becomes a real problem when you start talking about precision medicine. Sequencing a tumor is one thing. Connecting that information to treatment decisions and long-term outcomes is something else entirely.

This is something I have seen before in my work in machine learning. When systems aren’t seamless, evolution is clunky and muscle memory takes precedent.

Genomics have arrived at the edges

Sequencing is often discussed as something that is coming to veterinary oncology.

More accurately, it has already arrived at the edges. What comes next is routine clinical integration.

One study examined 828 tumors from 813 dogs across 53 cancer types. Nearly 90% contained mutations with potential diagnostic, prognostic or therapeutic implications.

That is exciting. But the word potential is doing a lot of work.

Finding a mutation is not the same as knowing what to do about it. Turning genomic information into better treatment decisions will require trials, usable workflows and evidence that those decisions actually improve outcomes.

Diagnostics and pathology are moving closer to the clinic

Another clear direction was diagnostics are moving closer to the veterinarian and the patient.

My sense is that vets want to be more empowered. They want answers faster and better tools they can use themselves, without having to send every question somewhere else and wait.

That doesn’t mean replacing specialists. It means bringing more of their knowledge into the clinic and making it available when the veterinarian needs it.

What is missing is not another AI model

AI was everywhere. The AVMA program included discussions about using AI, telemedicine and other technologies in everyday veterinary practice.

But a model is not a clinical product.

A clinical product needs a clear purpose, reliable data, external validation, known failure modes, human oversight and a way to fit into the existing workflow.

Most importantly, it needs evidence that it improves something meaningful. Veterinary medicine doesn’t need less AI. But it is crying out for proof that AI can make a significant difference to outcomes.

What this means for GAMGEE

For GAMGEE, my takeaway is that building revolutionary treatments is only part of the challenge. The bigger need is to turn scientific potential into real outcomes, and real outcomes into verifiable evidence. That is a huge lift, but also a huge opportunity.

The company that defines veterinary precision oncology will be the one that makes advanced biology usable in a real clinic, on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon, and proves that it improves outcomes.

That means building more than a vaccine or a sequencing report. It means building the workflow, the evidence, and the trust around it.

A lot to build.
